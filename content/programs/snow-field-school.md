The CUAHSI Snow Measurement Field School (co-organized with SINTER and supported by NASA) provides hands-on training in snow measurement methods for researchers across career stages. The program runs for five days each January at a mountain location that changes from year to year and is designed for graduate students, postdocs, professionals, and scientists who will make snow measurements as part of their research or who use snowpack data in their modeling and remote sensing work.

## What participants learn

The curriculum assumes little prior experience and progresses systematically through the week:

- **Snow safety and field practices** — essential foundations for working safely in winter environments
- **Basic measurements** — snow depth, density, and snow water equivalent using Federal snow samplers, avalanche probes, Magnaprobes, and related equipment
- **Snow pit analysis** — excavating and preparing pits; measuring profiles of density, temperature, grain size and shape, hardness, and stratigraphy
- **Sampling design** — designing field campaigns with specific scientific objectives; considerations for spatial variability and temporal sampling
- **Remote sensing context** — understanding how field measurements serve as ground truth for satellite-derived snowpack products
- **Student-designed campaigns** — by the end of the week, student teams design and execute their own mini field campaigns

Daylight hours are spent almost entirely in the field. Evenings include data analysis, reflection, and lectures on advanced topics.

## Eligibility

The program is aimed at graduate students, postdocs, professionals, and senior scientists who work with snow measurements or snowpack data in their research. Priority is given to graduate students and early-career researchers. No prior snow measurement experience is required, but participants must be physically able to spend full days active in winter field conditions.

Undergraduate students may participate on a space-available basis.

## Logistics

The field school is hosted at a mountain facility, and the location changes from year to year: past sessions have been held in the Sierra Nevada, the Rocky Mountains and the northeastern United States, and each year's location is announced with its application cycle. Housing, meals, and local transportation are provided on-site; participants share rooms. Registration costs are announced with each year's application cycle.

The program is competitive — applications are reviewed by workshop organizers and instructors and space is limited. Applications are typically reviewed on a rolling basis beginning in September for the following January program.

## Track record

The 2026 field school brought together 25 students and 7 instructors, including participants from the US Forest Service and multiple research universities. Highlights included visits to a SNOTEL monitoring site and the Fraser Experimental Forest. Student feedback has consistently highlighted the value of the professional network built during the week alongside the technical training.
